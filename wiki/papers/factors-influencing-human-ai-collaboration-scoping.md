## Problem & Context

As AI systems increasingly serve as partners rather than mere tools across work, education, and everyday life contexts, understanding what factors make human-AI collaboration effective has become critical. Despite a growing body of empirical literature, research on this topic had not been systematically synthesized. The field lacked a structured map of (1) how researchers conceptualize human-AI collaboration and (2) which factors empirically influence collaboration outcomes. Prior work tended to focus narrowly on system characteristics (accuracy, performance) or individual psychological constructs (trust, technology acceptance), without integrating these into a unified framework spanning the full range of contextual conditions.

## Key idea

Through a scoping review of 20 peer-reviewed studies (domestic and international, 2014–2024), the paper synthesizes the perspectives and factors that shape human-AI collaboration. Three conceptual framings of collaboration emerge from the reviewed literature: (1) AI as a tool, (2) task division between human and AI, and (3) complementary collaborative activity (horizontal, mutual). Across those framings, 96 individual factors are identified and grouped into four macro-categories ordered by frequency: AI-related factors (44.79%), human characteristics (28.13%), human-AI relationship factors (15.63%), and socio-environmental factors (11.46%). The review highlights that effective collaboration is not solely determined by AI capabilities; human psychological states, prior experience, competence, and the relational and environmental context are equally essential.

## Method

The study applies a structured scoping review methodology (5-stage protocol following Arksey & O'Malley and subsequent adaptations): (1) research question development, (2) systematic literature search, (3) paper selection, (4) data extraction, and (5) analysis, summary, and reporting. Stage 6 (expert consultation) was intentionally omitted as optional.

**Search scope**: RISS (domestic), Web of Science, Taylor & Francis, ScienceDirect, ProQuest Central, Wiley Online Library, SpringerLink (international). Keywords combined (AI OR Artificial Intelligence) AND (Human) AND (Collaboration OR Interaction OR Teamwork). Period: March 2014 – April 2024.

**Selection**: From 1,564 initial records, 274 duplicates were removed, 960 excluded on title/abstract screening, 310 excluded at full-text review, leaving 20 final papers. Exclusions targeted: papers without explicitly discussed factors, discourse studies lacking systematic research process, and simple AI-use studies not meeting the collaboration threshold.

**Analysis**: The 20 papers were coded for research context (work/education/everyday life/literature), research population, methodology type (quantitative/qualitative/mixed), their conceptualization of human-AI collaboration, and each individual factor influencing collaboration. Factor categories were derived inductively and cross-validated by the full research team plus two education PhDs.

## Experiment & Results

The review characterizes the 20 studies as follows:
- **Context**: Work situations 7 (35%), Education 6 (30%), Everyday life 5 (25%), Literature review 2 (10%).
- **Sample size**: 11–100 persons was most common (36.4%); 101–500 (31.8%); ≥500 (13.6%); <10 (9.1%); literature (9.1%).
- **Age**: Adults (general) 40.9%, university students 31.8%, adult workers 13.6%, elementary students 4.5%, literature 9.1%.
- **Method**: Quantitative 55%, mixed 25%, qualitative 20%. Experimental design dominated quantitative (40% of all 20).

**Collaboration framings** (Table 1):
- AI as a tool: 7 studies (35%) — AI used instrumentally; anthropomorphism effects on interaction studied.
- Task division (human + AI complementary roles): 5 studies (25%) — AI provides analysis/evidence, human decides.
- Complementary collaborative activities: 8 studies (40%) — horizontal, mutual collaboration with shifting initiative.

**Factors influencing human-AI collaboration** (96 total, Table 2):
1. AI-related factors: 43 (44.79%) — AI features/functionality 20 (20.83%), AI anthropomorphism 9 (9.38%), AI transparency 7 (7.29%), AI accuracy 7 (7.29%).
2. Human characteristics: 27 (28.13%) — user psychological characteristics 11 (11.46%), user perception 7 (7.29%), prior knowledge/experience 3 (3.13%), user competencies 3 (3.13%), physical characteristics 2 (2.08%).
3. Human-AI relationship factors: 15 (15.62%) — trust/reliability 6 (6.25%), human-AI relationship 4 (4.16%), human-AI mutual agreement 3 (3.13%), task division 2 (2.08%).
4. Socio-environmental factors: 11 (11.46%) — usage situation & environment 6 (6.25%), technology accessibility & cost 3 (3.13%), organizational collaboration & leadership 2 (2.08%).

The AI-related category dominates (44.79%), consistent with prior findings that effective collaboration requires users to understand AI characteristics. However, the review also emphasizes that human factors and contextual conditions are indispensable — positive effects of human-AI collaboration do not universally apply across all contexts.

## Limitations

- The scoping review method is designed for breadth (mapping evidence) rather than depth (meta-analytic synthesis of effect sizes). No statistical aggregation of effect magnitudes is provided.
- Expert consultation (Stage 6) was omitted, potentially reducing external validity of category labels.
- The 20 final papers represent a highly selective sample (20 out of 1,564). Studies that did not explicitly discuss "factors" were excluded, which may underrepresent context-specific or exploratory collaboration research.
- The domestic search was limited to RISS; other Korean databases (KISS, DBPIA) were not included, possibly missing relevant Korean-language studies.
- The paper does not distinguish between factors found to promote vs. impede collaboration — only frequency counts are reported.
- Coverage ends at April 2024; rapidly evolving large-language-model-era research is not captured.

## Open questions

- How do the relative weights of the four factor categories shift across context types (work vs. education vs. everyday)? Cross-tabulation was not provided.
- What is the interaction between AI-related factors and human characteristics — do high AI transparency and user competence compound or substitute for each other?
- As generative AI systems gain agentic capability, do new factor categories (e.g., goal alignment, initiative negotiation) emerge that are not captured by existing studies?
- How can the 96 individual factors be operationalized into design guidelines for human-AI collaborative systems?

## My take

The paper provides a useful organized snapshot of where empirical human-AI collaboration research stood as of early 2024. The four-category taxonomy (AI, human, relational, socio-environmental) is intuitive and coherent. The main limitation is methodological: scoping reviews map evidence but cannot rank factors by causal importance. The finding that AI-related factors dominate (44.79%) likely reflects researcher selection bias — studies tend to manipulate the system side because it is more controllable — rather than necessarily reflecting relative causal importance in practice. The three conceptual framings of collaboration (tool, task division, complementary) are a useful organizing contribution for future study design. For empirical human-AI research teams, this review usefully flags that relational and socio-environmental factors are systematically understudied (only 15.6% and 11.5% of total factors) despite their likely importance in real deployment contexts.
