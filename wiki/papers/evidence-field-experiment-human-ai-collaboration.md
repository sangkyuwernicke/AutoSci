## Problem & Context

AI systems increasingly match or exceed human experts in prediction tasks, prompting businesses and policymakers to debate whether to offer pure-AI or human-AI collaborative services. Regulatory pressure (EU GDPR Article 22, EU AI Act Article 14) mandates human oversight in high-risk automated decision-making. Yet almost no empirical work examines the downstream *consumption-side* effects of keeping a human in the loop: do end consumers respond differently to advice that passed through a human banker, even if the advice quality is identical? Prior work on human-AI production (Stage 1 of advice generation) shows mixed results — sometimes humans degrade AI advice via algorithm aversion, sometimes they improve it — but the effect on how customers *use* the advice is unexplored.

## Key idea

Human involvement in AI-based financial advisory acts as a **peripheral cue** (in the sense of Petty & Cacioppo's Elaboration Likelihood Model) that boosts customers' emotional trust and consequently their advice-taking, especially under high investment uncertainty — without consumers inferring any actual quality improvement from the human-AI complementarity. The paper separates production-side effects (do humans change the advice?) from consumption-side effects (do customers follow the advice differently?), and uses a Human-only condition to tease apart cognitive (central-route) from affective (peripheral-route) mechanisms.

## Method

**Stage 1 — Advice production:** 27 experienced bankers at a large German savings bank each reviewed 10 of 24 personal loan opportunities generated by a neural-network credit-default model (ROC-AUC 0.71). Each banker first gave unaided recommendations, then updated them after seeing the AI's risk assessment. The final human-AI advice was the modal recommendation across all bankers per loan.

**Stage 2 — Field experiment (N=130 customers):** Bank customers who visited branches were endowed with €100 per loan and assigned via between-subjects randomization to receive advice labeled as (a) AI-only (baseline), (b) Human-AI collaboration (main treatment), or (c) Human-only (the same human-AI advice but without disclosing AI's involvement). Customers made investment decisions and risk assessments before and after receiving advice across 10 loan opportunities. Outcome: `FinalAlign` (binary: does final investment decision match the advice?). Analysis: OLS panel regressions with loan-by-advice fixed effects and clustered standard errors.

**Online controlled experiment (N=87 participants via Prolific):** Replicated field design with uniform advice across conditions (eliminates advice-content confound), added direct measurement of cognitive trust, emotional trust, decision autonomy, shared responsibility, and prior beliefs about advisor accuracy. Multilevel moderated mediation (Model 7; lavaan in R) tested whether uncertainty moderated emotional trust as a mediator of the treatment → advice-alignment path.

## Experiment & Results

**Production side:** Bankers significantly changed the AI advice (GapRiskAssess > 0, p < 0.001; InvestAlign < 1, p < 0.001) but average investment recommendation accuracy did not significantly differ from AI-only (Δ = -0.03, p = 0.23), confirming human involvement did not compromise quality in this setting.

**Field experiment — main effect:** Human-AI vs. AI-only raises FinalAlign by **+15.5 percentage points** (OLS with customer-clustered SE, significant at p < 0.001). Effect robust across logistic specification, branch fixed effects, and subsample of loans with identical advice across conditions.

**Human-only vs. Human-AI:** No significant difference in FinalAlign, ruling out that the gain is driven by consumers expecting a quality boost from two-agent complementarity (challenging the central-route explanation).

**Uncertainty moderation:** Treatment effect concentrated in high-risk loans (advisor risk assessment > median of 4/7): positive and significant. No significant effect for low-risk loans. Consistent with ELM: peripheral route dominates under uncertainty.

**Welfare effect:** Customers under Human-AI earned on average **+44.9%** higher final payoffs than AI-only customers (log-payoff OLS); effect driven by the high-risk subsample.

**Online experiment:** Replicates +9.2% FinalAlign gap (p = 0.08 without covariates; statistically significant after controls). Neither prior beliefs about accuracy nor disappointment in accuracy explains the gap. Moderated mediation confirms: uncertainty × human involvement → emotional trust (peripheral route) → FinalAlign (indirect effect = 0.040, 95% CI [0.005, 0.076], p < 0.05); no analogous effect via cognitive trust (central route). The moderated indirect effect is significant (0.056, 95% CI [0.006, 0.105]).

## Limitations

- Single financial domain (personal loan investment) at one German savings bank — generalizability to other verticals (medical, legal) or cultures is assumed but not tested.
- No genuine human-only condition in Stage 2: the "Human-only" arm conceals AI involvement, so Human-AI vs. pure-human comparison is unavailable.
- AI and human-AI advice accuracy both ~70% (mid-range); the welfare benefit depends critically on advice quality — in low-quality AI settings, human-induced reliance could harm consumers.
- Small sample: 130 field customers and 87 online participants limit statistical power for subgroup analyses.
- Advice accuracy disclosed uniformly at 70% in the online experiment; this may not reflect real-world settings where accuracy is unknown.
- Decision confidence items for cognitive trust had low Cronbach α (0.41), limiting measurement precision.

## Open questions

- Does the peripheral-route mechanism persist when AI is disclosed to have much higher accuracy (e.g., 90%) than the human banker?
- How does higher human advisor confidence or displayed confidence scores affect the peripheral vs. central route balance?
- Do the effects replicate in higher-stakes domains (wealth management, medical diagnosis) where emotional reassurance may be amplified?
- What happens when human-AI collaboration produces lower-quality advice — does over-reliance induced by peripheral cues lead to consumer harm, and how can firms mitigate this?
- Can the peripheral-cue mechanism be replicated cross-culturally, especially in low-trust institutional environments?

## My take

The core contribution is the clean separation of production-side vs. consumption-side effects in a real-world RCT with incentivized decisions — rare in IS/management research. The finding that human involvement boosts advice-taking via affective trust rather than quality inference is practically important: it means firms do not need to actually improve advice quality to gain compliance benefits from human oversight, but this is a double-edged sword for consumer welfare. The paper is careful to flag the welfare caveat (only beneficial when advice quality is high). The multilevel moderated mediation adds mechanistic rigor beyond typical field studies. Main limitation is the small N and single-context scope.
